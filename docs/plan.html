N6 Demo Plan

A twelve-screen touch demo for the STM32N6570-DK: live dashboards, dense real-time data visualization, full-screen video with sound, and a live camera with hand tracking on the NPU, all locked to the panel refresh. Built for people who look for dropped frames, banding and fake data. Every number on screen is measured on the board or arrives over its Ethernet link, and the board now records its own screen and sound.

This version: 2026-11-19, after the video (V4) was rendered and approved. It is made from the lap the board recorded (V3); the recording stays in captures/rec and the renders in media/out, never committed.

A video of one attract lap, made by the board

A video for YouTube: one attract lap (2 min 38 s with its end titles, set to the film's music since 2026-11-18) exactly as the panel shows it, frame for frame and with the board's own stereo sound, recorded by the board's own H.264 encoder. Titles and captions carry it; there is no narration and no narrator. The lap is the real demo at real speed with live data, not a re-enactment; its four bars of hand tracking are the one replay, and say so on screen.

Capturing it: the board records itself (built)

  • The N6's hardware H.264 encoder (VENC). The recorder calls ST's encoder library (BSD-3-Clause, fetched with the HAL) directly with RGB888 input, so the frame buffer on screen is the input: no conversion, no copy. Zephyr's driver only takes NV12 or RGB565.
  • A cooperative thread wakes at the start of each refresh's active area and encodes the buffer on screen every second refresh (30 fps; 60 when the client asks) in about 7 ms, 9 to 17 ms during video playback. The UI does not draw into that buffer until the encoder lets go; so far it has not had to wait.
  • The sound is a tap on the DAC feed: bit-exact, no cable. Each picture carries the DAC frame that was playing when it appeared, so tools/record.py places every picture on the sound's clock and writes MKV (PCM) and MP4 (AAC).
  • Two fixes the encoder needed, both link-time wraps that leave the Zephyr tree alone: the data cache is written back at the moment the hardware starts (cleaned earlier or invalidated after, the stream lost its headers), and the library's memory is allocated outside Zephyr's driver, whose allocation table never shrinks and corrupted memory after a few encoder starts. That bug is worth reporting upstream.
  • The first sync measurement found the board's own video player showing each picture 33 ms after its sound. It now decodes 30 ms ahead, and the sync clip's flashes land -8 to +13 ms from their beeps.

Home power, when the internet is there

The user's idea: when the board can reach the internet, a tile appears on Home that opens a panel of the household's electricity, like the power part of the user's energy-monitor dashboard (not its gas or heating, not its tech stack). Built the same day as Home Power, the thirteenth screen, in the dashboard's own Notch skin (the user's choice over its Scope skin and over the demo's theme). The monitor's address is set on the board (ctl.py "power url http://HOST", kept on the card) and not written into this repository.

What the board does

  • A feed thread of its own (its 6 KB stack in PSRAM) looks the name up with Zephyr's DNS resolver, now in prj.conf (the server from DHCP, or the gateway with a fixed address), and sends plain HTTP/1.0 requests; a small JSON walker picks the figures out of the answers in a 64 KB PSRAM buffer. Nothing on the UI thread waits for the network.
  • /api/stats every minute, every 15 s while the screen or Home shows (the Home tile is the panel's gauge at half size, live: the user's request after seeing the first version, which had the last hour's trace); the panel's range (1h to 30d) every 15 s, or every minute for 7d and 30d, while it shows. /api/stream is not used.
  • Online once a request is answered, offline after two failures in a row. The screen's new listed hook gives it a Home tile, a page dot and a place in the edge swipes only while online; the shell now holds 16 screens.

Measured (2026-11-19)

  • A request 180 to 480 ms (the first includes the name lookup and the connection); every range's figures match the monitor's own.
  • The screen at rest 1.1 ms a frame (it redraws when figures arrive); its first frame about 10 ms, 13 ms on the first visit after boot, the chart a frame later (3.9 ms); the 30-day chart 3.1 ms to draw.
  • With an address that does not resolve, the tile, dot and swipe place go away and the screen greys out like the dashboard's stale state; with a good one they come back.
  • The full tour with it: 0 dropped frames everywhere, Home's pan test with 17 tiles. The tile's gauge costs Home 0.29 ms a frame while it is in view.
  • The cost: internal RAM 3.4 KB left (was 10 KB), almost all of it the DNS resolver; the app image 1.997 of 2.09 MB.

The microSD card: at full speed, and taken out and put back

Found while looking at internal RAM: the card's bus ran at 48 MHz in Default Speed mode, which the SD specification allows only to 25 MHz, because nothing ever switched the card to High Speed (CMD6). It runs at 24 MHz now, within the specification; measured with a 48.7 MB upload, the card writes 5.4 MB/s there against 9.0 MB/s at 48 MHz (uploads 2.7 against 3.65 MB/s; playback needs about 1 MB/s either way).

1. High Speed at 48 MHz done

  • A linker wrap around HAL_SD_Init in sdcard.c: the card comes up at 24 MHz, is switched to High Speed with the HAL's own HAL_SD_ConfigSpeedBusOperation, and only if it agrees does the 4-bit setup that follows run the bus at 48 MHz. A card that refuses stays at 24 MHz.
  • Every SDHC and SDXC card supports High Speed; the faster UHS-I modes need 1.8 V signalling, which this board does not switch to.
  • Done when: CLKCR shows the undivided clock, uploads are back to their old speed, checksums verify, and the tour is clean. Met on 2026-11-19: the card accepted the switch ("High Speed at 48 MHz"), CLKCR 0x00024000, uploads 3.46 and 3.56 MB/s (2.71 at 24 MHz), card seeks 13 to 31 ms again (88 ms at 24 MHz), 0 dropped frames in the tour.

2. A card taken out and put back while the demo runs done

  • Today a card put in after boot is mounted only on request (n6v.py mount), and one pulled while mounted is lost until a restart; a clip playing from it, the attract lap's among them, would most likely hold.
  • The plan: watch the card-detect pin (debounced). On removal close a clip playing from the card, unmount, deinitialise the disk; the card's clips leave Cinema's list (flash copies take their place) and attract mode falls back to the screens taking turns. On insertion initialise (through the same wrap, so at High Speed again), mount, rescan and rebuild the gallery off the UI thread.
  • Done when: pulling and replacing the card during playback, during the attract lap and during an upload leaves no hung screen or thread, the clips come back, and the frame and underrun counts stay at 0. Met on 2026-11-19 with the card pulled by hand: the clip closed in 0.15 to 0.22 s, the card was back 0.52 to 1.17 s after it went in, at High Speed, 0 dropped frames (audio underruns not measured in that run; the tour now acts the removals out and counts them: 0). The first round found one more trap (after a write cut off, the disk's reference count stayed up and no mount initialised the card again): fixed with a forced deinit after every unmount and failed mount.
  • Also: every file stays whole whatever the moment (clips replaced via name.old, settings via name.new, an unfinished upload deleted at the next mount), and ctl.py "sdcard out|in" acts a removal out for tests without hands.

The four Google models are the ones ST ships in its N6 hand and face applications. SFace as published ran almost half its steps on the CPU, because the NPU has no PReLU activation: each PReLU is rewritten exactly as a·x + (1−a)·ReLU(x) from operators the NPU runs (outputs equal to within 10−6), then the model is re-quantized to per-channel int8. The few CPU steps left in each network are input scaling and quantization at its edges.

Hands, as built

  • The palm detector runs on the letterboxed camera picture: every frame while no hand is tracked (about 65 ms), every 500 ms with one. The landmark network places 21 points from a 224 x 224 crop turned upright around each hand (the crop about 9 ms of CPU, the network about 22 ms).
  • Up to two hands, each in its own track. A detection counts as a new hand only away from every tracked palm, so one hand is never tracked twice. One hand runs at 26 to 30 fps, two at 15 fps.
  • Gestures from joint angles, at any rotation and for either hand: fist, open hand, pointing, victory, thumbs up and down, OK, rock on, call me, I love you, a finger count and more. Left and right labels checked by the user on the panel.
  • Both networks give the original models' answers on the PC (landmarks within 0.9 px).

Faces, designed, not built

  • Every frame: BlazeFace, then the 468-point mesh for each face (precise eyes for alignment; the mesh can be drawn).
  • Identification when a face first appears, about once a second to confirm, and when a better view arrives. Faces are followed between frames, so names stay on them.
  • LEARN takes about five good aligned views and averages their signatures; matching is cosine similarity against a threshold calibrated on public same-person and different-person pairs. Below it: "unknown".
  • Quantization is calibrated with public face crops kept on the PC; the int8 model is checked against the float one (signature agreement, verification accuracy) before it reaches the board.

A theremin on Signal Lab: the spectrum analyser you can play

Signal Lab's third source, THEREMIN, turns the spectrum into the instrument. A fingertip on it sounds a tone whose frequency is the spectrum's own frequency at that column (the same log axis, 20 Hz to 20 kHz) and whose level is the spectrum's own level at that height. The analyser then shows the tone it is playing, so the peak sits under the finger, follows it when it slides, and drops with it. The scope shows the waveform, the waterfall writes the melody as it scrolls. Up to five fingers play five voices.

Playing

  • Horizontal: frequency, sig_colf_hz(col), so the tone lands exactly on the axis under the finger. The field stops short of the screen edges, where the shell's edge swipes start, so changing screens still works.
  • Vertical: level on a loudness taper: -6 dBFS at the top, 10 dB less per halving of the height (the user's choice: even dB steps left the bottom half inaudible). The analyser's level axis uses the same taper in this mode. The spectrum takes 252 px of the pane and the waterfall a 58 px strip.
  • Each finger gets a crosshair through spectrum and waterfall and a readout beside it ("A4 440.0 Hz -18 dB"), drawn in the pane's draw handler, not as label objects.
  • WAVE cycles the timbre, each with its own picture on the log spectrum: sine, theremin (slightly overdriven, a little vibrato), triangle, sawtooth (band-limited, so nothing folds back). SNAP glides the pitch to the nearest semitone and shows note ticks.
  • SOUND off plays on screen in silence. A finger slid off the field keeps its level until it lifts; one that comes down off the field does not play.

Sound

  • A synthesizer in dsp/theremin.c is the audio output's source: five phase-continuous oscillators in 128-frame blocks (2.7 ms), so a frequency change never steps the waveform.
  • Every change is a ramp: pitch glides in log frequency, levels ramp, a touch fades in over 10 ms, a lift decays over about 150 ms. Voices are summed through a soft limiter (ceiling -0.9 dBFS), so five loud fingers never clip.
  • The synthesizer reads the touch state itself at the start of each block on the audio thread: touch to sound 3.8 to 4.8 ms (11 to 21 ms with the earlier 512-frame blocks), shown on screen as a measured figure.
  • Every sample it plays also goes into a ring that Signal Lab analyses, so the picture is the sound that reaches the DAC.
  • The DAC's clock runs at 47.7 kHz, not 48 (measured; PLL2 from the HSI): oscillators and analysis both use the measured rate, so A4 is 440 Hz in the air and on the axis.

Listening to the microphone through an equalizer you drag

On the replacement DK the onboard microphone works; the first board's delivered noise only (the investigation is in docs/microphone.md). With MICROPHONE selected, Signal Lab sends it to the headphones through a five-band parametric equalizer drawn on its own spectrum, to judge the microphone by ear and shape it with the fingers.

Using it

  • The header's middle boxes become LISTEN, GAIN (+20, +30 or +40 dB) and EQ (tap: in or bypassed; hold: all bands flat).
  • Five handles on the spectrum: low shelf, three peaks, high shelf. Across is frequency, up and down is gain on the equalizer's own ±15 dB scale with a detent at 0; a second finger near a held handle sets the band's width.
  • The spectrum shows what is heard, after the equalizer, with the curve drawn over it.
  • LISTEN on counts as someone using the demo: attract mode does not take the screen from a listener who is not touching it.

The sound path

  • Microphone and DAC run on one clock (the SAI's 12.288 MHz), so the monitor reads the microphone a fixed 160 samples behind its newest sample, and that distance never moves.
  • 8.7 ms from microphone to DAC with 128-frame blocks; 35 ms with the earlier 512-frame blocks, which is why the blocks shrank.
  • Simper's state-variable filters in float, with the Audio EQ Cookbook's responses, eased every 32 samples; a limiter at -6 dBFS. Double-precision biquads had cost 0.75 ms per 512-frame block; the float filters take 75 us, 133 us at worst in the tour.
  • Closed loop: a tone from headphones lying by the board, measured in the microphone before and after the equalizer, matches the drawn curve within 0.2 dB.
  • By ear, the user: excellent fidelity, very acceptable latency.

Why Zephyr + LVGL

Zephyr 4.4.0 already describes this board's display, touch, Ethernet, PSRAM and flash in its devicetree, and the workspace was on this machine and known to boot. LVGL 9.5 supplies layout, text and input handling, which leaves the effort for custom drawing where it shows.

What was set aside, and why

  • TouchGFX. Strong on this chip, but driven from a GUI designer that cannot be scripted from here.
  • Software H.264 decode. The chip encodes H.264 but has no decoder; clips are Motion JPEG for the hardware codec.
  • Zephyr's display write. It cleaned the cache over the whole 1.5 MB buffer by address (541 us) and swapped mid-blanking.
  • Zephyr's I2S driver. It restarts the DMA from an interrupt at every block, which the SAI's 83 us FIFO does not survive.
  • Zephyr's input queue. Its 16 events dropped touch releases under load; callbacks now run in the driver's context.
Display

One refresh, and where a frame goes in it

The app owns both frame buffers in internal SRAM. LVGL renders straight into the back buffer, and present() hands it to the LTDC itself: cleaned from the data cache by set and way (25 us), swapped at once if the panel is in its vertical blanking, otherwise reloaded at the start of the next blanking. A frame handed over in the blanking still shows in the next refresh.

One refresh at 59.98 Hz: 501 lines, 33.3 us each16.67 ms
Blanking, lines 492 to 11 0.70 ms: a buffer swapped here shows in the next refresh Active area, lines 12 to 491 15.97 ms: the panel scans the front buffer; the recorder starts reading it at line 12
  • Dense pictures bypass LVGL's widgets. A draw handler gets the layer buffer and writes pixels: spectra, waterfalls, wireframes, 3D posts and ribbons. Moving text is drawn there too, since LVGL redraws the whole screen once a frame has more than 32 invalid areas.
  • Transitions cost no drawing. They fade with the layer's alpha and settle by moving the layer's window.
  • The Camera screen runs single-buffered. The second buffer is lent to the NPU; every frame starts at the top of the refresh and the camera picture is drawn first, by DMA2D, to stay ahead of the scanout.
  • Counted, not assumed. Late swaps, the margin before each deadline, the time a screen switch takes, and per-frame traces of the last transition and the last dropped frame are on the control port.

Method

Build loop and verification

Getting code onto the board

n6.cmd go builds, power-cycles the target through the ST-LINK, writes the flash over SWD, power-cycles again and starts it, with the board left in development boot. A source change is about two minutes; a configuration change rebuilds LVGL in six to eight. With BOOT1 at L the board starts the demo by itself.

How I check a screen I cannot see

  • Screenshots of the frame buffer over Ethernet, looked at after every UI change.
  • The tour (tools/tour.py, about seven minutes): every screen, injected gestures, transitions, video from flash and card, an upload, recorded sound, an attract lap. 0 dropped frames everywhere before any commit.
  • The soak (tools/soak.py): attract and camera phases for hours, the board polled every second for resets, leaks and drift.
  • Sound by numbers: the DAC feed recorded and its discontinuities counted; a test tone through headphones by the board and back into the microphone; flash against beep in the board's own recording.
  • NPU against the PC: both networks' outputs on the board compared with the original models.

What needed your eyes and ears

Checked by the user on the panel: touch, navigation, motion and colour (2026-11-14); sound, transitions and attract mode (2026-11-15); the slider fix and five-finger touch (2026-11-15); hand tracking with both hands (2026-11-16); the theremin and listening to the microphone (2026-11-17); the recorded lap (2026-11-18); the video (2026-11-19). Risk

What could go wrong, and what did

RiskOutcomeWhat happened
From the original plan
Hands-free load failsretiredA power cycle through the ST-LINK before flashing and before starting fixed it: ST's loader leaves the flash in octal mode, which a pin reset does not clear.
PSRAM too slow for 32-bit frameshappened85 MB/s copies against 361 in internal SRAM. Both 32-bit buffers moved to internal SRAM, which left the app 840 KB.
JPEG decode pathretiredThe ST HAL, called directly: about 10 ms a frame. One trap: the input has to be padded to whole words past the end marker.
Audio output wiringretiredUnderruns until the DMA buffer moved to AHBSRAM, the sleep clocks were enabled, and one circular DMA through the HAL replaced Zephyr's I2S driver. 0 underruns since.
MCUboot execute-in-placeretiredWorks as documented; the slots grew to 2 MB for the NPU's programs.
LVGL too slowretiredDense visuals draw straight into the buffer; every screen is under budget.
